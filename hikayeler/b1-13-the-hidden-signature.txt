The Hidden Signature
B1

For her final project at art school, Deniz chose to copy a painting. It was a small, forgotten picture in the corner of the city museum: a woman sitting by a window, reading a letter, with soft yellow light on her face. The label said only: "Unknown artist, around 1890."

Nobody ever stopped in front of it. Deniz had loved it since she was a child.

She spent three months in the museum with her easel and paints, studying every detail. She learned how the unknown artist had mixed colours, how they had built up the light in thin layers, how they had painted the woman's hands with just a few quick lines. The museum guard, an elderly man called Mr Veli, started bringing her coffee in the mornings.

One afternoon, while she was looking very closely at the bottom corner of the painting, she noticed something strange. Under the dark paint of the floor, there were some faint lines. They looked like letters.

She told her teacher, who told the museum director. At first, the director was not interested. "Students always think they've discovered something," he said. But Deniz insisted, and finally the museum agreed to examine the painting with special lights.

The X-ray images showed something nobody expected. Under the dark corner, there was a signature: "S. Avcı, 1891." And under the woman by the window, there was a completely different painting — a self-portrait of a young woman holding a paintbrush.

The museum's experts began to investigate. It took them almost a year. They found that S. Avcı was Saliha Avcı, a woman who had studied art in Paris in the 1880s. At that time, very few women were allowed to exhibit their work, and most galleries refused to show paintings by women. Saliha had painted over her own signature, probably so that the painting could be sold. Her name had disappeared from history.

But now, with her name, the researchers found more. Letters in an old archive. Two more paintings in private collections. A notebook full of sketches in a family house in İstanbul. Slowly, the story of a forgotten artist came back to life.

The next year, the museum opened a special exhibition: "Saliha Avcı: The Woman Behind the Window." The small painting was no longer in the corner. It was in the centre of the main room, with a new label and a spotlight.

Next to it, there was a smaller painting. It was Deniz's copy, the one that had started everything. Her teacher gave her the highest mark in the class.

On the opening night, Mr Veli brought her a coffee, as usual.

"Every day for twenty years, I walked past that painting," he said. "I never really looked at it. You looked."

Deniz smiled. "She was waiting a long time," she said. "Somebody just had to look closely."
