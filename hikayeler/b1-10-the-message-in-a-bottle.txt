The Message in a Bottle
B1

Elif found the bottle on a cold morning in February, half buried in the sand of a beach on the Black Sea coast. It was green glass, thick and scratched, with a cork in the top. Inside, she could see a rolled piece of paper.

It took her twenty minutes to get the cork out. The paper was dry, which surprised her. The message was written in English, in neat, careful handwriting:

"My name is Thomas. I am eleven years old and I live in a lighthouse in Scotland with my father. I am throwing this bottle into the sea on 14 August 1998. If you find it, please write to me and tell me where it travelled. I want to know how big the world is."

There was an address at the bottom: a lighthouse on a small Scottish island.

Elif read it several times. The bottle had been in the sea for more than twenty-five years. She wondered how it had travelled from the cold North Atlantic all the way to the Black Sea. Maybe through the Mediterranean, past Gibraltar, through the Bosphorus. Maybe it had been stuck on a beach somewhere for years before the sea took it again.

She knew the boy was not a boy anymore. Thomas would be in his late thirties now. The lighthouse probably didn't even have a keeper. But she wrote a letter anyway, explaining where and when she had found the bottle. She added a photograph of the beach and a small bag of black sand. Then she posted it, not really expecting an answer.

Three months later, an email arrived from an address she didn't recognise.

"Dear Elif," it began. "My name is Thomas MacLeod. I'm the boy from the bottle."

The lighthouse had been automated years ago, Thomas explained. His father had died, and Thomas now worked as a marine biologist, studying ocean currents. Somebody on the island had found her letter in the old lighthouse post box and sent it on to him.

"You will not believe this," he wrote. "I became a scientist because of that bottle. When I was eleven, I threw seven bottles into the sea. Nobody ever answered. I always wondered where they went, and that question became my job. I study exactly how things travel across the ocean. Now, after twenty-six years, I finally know where one of them went. My colleagues are very excited. Your bottle has travelled a route that we thought was almost impossible."

Thomas sent her a map showing the possible journey of the bottle. It had travelled more than eight thousand kilometres.

They kept writing to each other. The next summer, Thomas came to Turkey for a conference, and Elif showed him the beach where she had found it.

Before he left, he gave her a new bottle with a new message inside.

"Throw it whenever you want," he said. "And we'll see where the world takes it this time."
