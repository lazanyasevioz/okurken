The Glacier
B2

In 1984, Professor Ömer Tan stood on a rock in the Swiss Alps and took a photograph of his six-year-old daughter in front of a glacier. In the picture, little Ayşe is wearing a red jacket that is too big for her, laughing, with a wall of blue-white ice rising behind her like a frozen wave. The glacier fills the whole background of the photograph. You cannot see where it ends.

Forty years later, Ayşe Tan was a glaciologist herself, and she had come back to stand on the same rock.

It had taken her two days to find it. The landscape had changed so much that her father's old notes were almost useless. Paths had disappeared. Streams ran where there had once been ice. Finally, she recognised the rock by its shape — a strange, crooked thing that looked a little like a sleeping dog — and climbed onto it with her camera.

She lifted the old photograph and held it up against the view.

The glacier was gone.

Not completely. If she looked carefully, she could see a dirty grey tongue of ice far up the valley, perhaps two kilometres away, retreating into the shadows of the mountain. But the place where it had once stood, the place where a six-year-old girl had laughed in front of a frozen wave, was now a field of grey rocks and small, determined plants. A meltwater lake, milky turquoise, lay where the ice had been thickest.

Ayşe had studied this. She had published papers on it. She knew the numbers by heart: the Alps had lost more than half of their glacier volume since the 1930s, and the rate of loss was accelerating. She had presented graphs at international conferences and explained, calmly and professionally, what the data meant.

But the numbers had never felt like this. She sat down on the rock, and for a long time she couldn't do anything at all.

Her father had died two years earlier. In his last months, when his memory was failing, he had often asked about this place. "Is the ice still there?" he would ask. "Did you go back?" She had always told him that she would go soon, that she was busy, that next summer would be better.

She took the new photograph. Same rock, same angle, same mountain. A forty-six-year-old woman in a red jacket — she had bought it especially — standing in front of an empty valley. She didn't smile.

When she returned to the university, she did something unusual. Instead of writing another scientific paper, she put the two photographs side by side and posted them online with a short text. She didn't include any graphs. She just wrote about her father, about the girl in the red jacket, and about the strange grief of returning to a place and finding that it no longer exists.

The post was shared more than two million times.

Hundreds of people replied with their own pairs of photographs: a frozen lake in Canada where their grandparents had skated and which no longer froze; a beach in the Pacific that had disappeared under the sea; a snowy mountain in Turkey that was now brown in winter. Teachers used the images in their classrooms. A museum asked to exhibit them.

Some people, inevitably, attacked her. They said the photographs were fake, that glaciers had always changed, that scientists were exaggerating for money. Ayşe didn't argue with them. She simply posted the data, calmly, as she always had, next to the two pictures.

"I have spent twenty years trying to explain this with numbers," she said in an interview later. "I was wrong to think numbers were enough. People don't protect statistics. They protect places they love."

She now leads a project that collects "then and now" photographs from around the world, matched with scientific measurements. Thousands of families have contributed. Each pair of images is a small piece of evidence and a small piece of memory at the same time.

The first pair in the collection is hers. On the left, a laughing child in a red jacket. On the right, a woman standing alone in front of the rocks.

Underneath, there is only one line, in her father's handwriting, copied from the back of the original photograph:

"Ayşe and the glacier. Both growing."
