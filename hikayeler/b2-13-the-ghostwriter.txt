The Ghostwriter
B2

Nobody had ever heard of Clara Holt, which was exactly how she liked it. She had written fourteen bestselling books, and her name appeared on none of them.

Clara was a ghostwriter. Famous people — footballers, actors, politicians, chefs — paid her to write their autobiographies. She would spend weeks interviewing them, listening to their stories, studying the way they spoke. Then she would disappear for a few months and come back with a book that sounded exactly like them, only clearer, funnier and more honest than they could ever have written themselves. Their names went on the cover. Hers appeared, if at all, in small letters at the end of the acknowledgements: "Thanks to Clara, for her help."

She was good at it because she was good at listening, and because she had no particular desire to be noticed. She liked being invisible. It allowed her to see things.

Her fifteenth client was Victor Lang, one of the most famous television presenters in the country. He was charming, energetic and loved by millions of viewers for his warm, family-friendly Saturday evening show. His publisher wanted an autobiography full of happy anecdotes, inspirational lessons and funny stories about celebrities.

For the first few weeks, that was exactly what Clara got. Victor was a wonderful storyteller. He described his poor childhood, his lucky break, his loving family. But Clara noticed small things. The way his assistant flinched when he raised his voice. The way two of his former colleagues, whom she interviewed for background, became suddenly careful and vague when she mentioned his name. A story he told twice, in two different versions, with different people as the hero.

She began to dig, quietly, as ghostwriters are not supposed to do. Over two months, she spoke to eleven former employees of his show. Their stories were remarkably similar: years of bullying, humiliation and threats, hidden behind his public smile. Several had left the industry completely. None of them had ever spoken publicly, because he was powerful, and they were afraid.

Clara faced a choice she had never faced before. Her contract was clear. She was paid to write Victor Lang's version of Victor Lang's life. The publisher had already paid a large advance. If she refused, she would probably never work as a ghostwriter again. If she wrote the happy book, millions of people would read it and love him even more.

She wrote the happy book. It took her four months, and it was the best work she had ever done: funny, warm, beautifully structured. Victor loved it. The publisher loved it.

Then, on the night before it was sent to the printers, she wrote something else.

It was a long article, carefully researched, with the agreement of the eleven former employees, five of whom were willing to be named. She sent it to the most respected newspaper in the country. Then she wrote to the publisher and resigned.

The article was published two weeks later. For several days, it was the only thing anyone talked about. Victor Lang denied everything at first, then stopped answering questions. His television contract was suspended, and then cancelled. More people came forward, dozens of them. The autobiography was never published.

The consequences for Clara were mixed. As she had expected, the publishing world was nervous about her. Several agents stopped returning her calls. But other doors opened. The newspaper offered her a job as an investigative journalist. And for the first time in her career, she received letters from readers — not addressed to a celebrity, but to her.

One of them came from Victor Lang's former assistant, the one who had flinched.

"Thank you," it said, "for writing the true story, and for putting your own name on it."

Clara has not been invisible since. It was uncomfortable at first. She still prefers listening to talking. But she has discovered that sometimes, a writer's job is not to sound like someone else.

Sometimes, it is to give a voice to the people who were never allowed to have one.
