The Second Race
B1

Twenty years ago, Murat Kaplan was the fastest young runner in the country. At the national championship, he was leading the final race by ten metres when he fell on the last bend. He broke his ankle in two places. By the time it healed, his chance was gone. He never ran professionally again.

Now he was forty, worked in an insurance office, and coached the athletics team at his daughter's school on Thursday afternoons. Nobody at the school knew his story. He preferred it that way.

There was one girl on the team who interested him. Her name was Zeynep, and she was thirteen. She wasn't the fastest, and she wasn't the strongest. But she never stopped. When the others walked during training, she kept running. When she lost, she asked what she had done wrong. She reminded him of someone he used to know.

In the spring, there was an important regional race. Zeynep trained every day. On the morning of the race, she was so nervous that she couldn't eat.

"What if I fall?" she asked him at the starting line.

Murat thought for a long time before answering. Then he told her his story, the whole thing. The bend, the fall, the ankle, the years of being angry at himself.

"For a long time, I thought that fall was the end of everything," he said. "It wasn't. It was just the end of one race. You can fall today. You can lose. And tomorrow, you'll still be a runner if you decide to be one."

Zeynep looked at him with wide eyes. Then she nodded and walked to the start.

The race was 1500 metres. For the first three laps, Zeynep stayed in the middle of the group. On the last lap, she started to move forward. On the final bend, the same kind of bend where Murat had fallen, she was in second place. A girl from another school tried to pass her, their feet touched, and Zeynep fell.

Murat stood up. The whole stadium was silent.

Zeynep lay on the track for one second. Then she got up. Her knee was bleeding. Six runners had passed her. She started running again, and she did not stop until she crossed the line, in seventh place.

She was crying when she reached him, but she was smiling too. "I fell," she said. "And I finished."

"I know," said Murat. His voice was not very steady. "That's more than I did."

That evening, for the first time in twenty years, Murat put on his old running shoes and went for a run alone through the quiet streets. His ankle hurt a little. He didn't care.

The next Thursday, the team found a new rule written on the board in the changing room: "Falling is allowed. Staying down is not."
