The Inheritance
B2

The three Aydemir siblings had not been in the same room for nine years when they met at their father's house to divide what he had left them.

There was Kemal, the eldest, a successful lawyer who had moved abroad and who arrived in an expensive rental car, already talking about property values. There was Nil, the middle child, a teacher who had lived ten minutes away from their father and looked after him during his long illness, and who had not forgiven her brothers for visiting only twice. And there was Sinan, the youngest, a musician who had never had much money and who had argued with their father so badly at twenty-two that they had barely spoken for the rest of his life.

The house itself was the main problem. It was old, falling apart, and located in a part of the city that had recently become fashionable. Kemal wanted to sell it immediately; a developer had already made an offer. Nil wanted to keep it. Sinan said he didn't care, which neither of the others believed.

They argued for most of the first morning. Old accusations returned with surprising speed: who had been the favourite, who had abandoned whom, who had ruined which family holiday in 1998. By lunchtime, Nil was in tears, Kemal was on the phone to his office, and Sinan had gone into the garden to smoke, a habit he had quit five years earlier.

In the afternoon, they began to sort through their father's things in silence. There were decades of newspapers, broken radios he had always planned to repair, and a drawer full of keys that didn't open anything. Then, in the back of the wardrobe in his bedroom, Nil found a large metal box. It was locked.

One of the useless keys opened it.

Inside were three folders, one for each of them, labelled in their father's careful handwriting. Each folder was full of documents. Kemal's contained every newspaper article that had ever mentioned his legal cases, carefully cut out and dated, including some from foreign papers that their father must have searched for at the library. Nil's contained every school report and every thank-you card from her students that she had ever shown him, together with notes he had made after each of her visits during his illness: what she had brought, what they had talked about, how tired she had looked.

Sinan's folder was the thickest. It contained tickets. Concert tickets, dozens of them, from small bars and cultural centres all over the country, covering the last thirteen years. Each one had a date written on the back, and sometimes a short comment: "The new song was excellent." "Too loud, but he was happy." "He saw me. I left before the end."

Sinan stared at them for a long time.

"He came," he said finally. His voice was very strange. "He came to my concerts. All this time. He never said anything."

"He was too proud," said Nil quietly. "Like you."

At the bottom of the box, there was an envelope addressed to all three of them. Kemal opened it and read it out loud. It was short.

"I was not very good at saying things. I hope this box says them for me. I have been proud of each of you in different ways, and I did not tell you, which was a mistake I made every day for many years. Please do not fight about the house. It is only a house. The important thing in it was always you three, around the kitchen table, arguing. I would like you to sit there one more time."

They sat at the kitchen table that evening. Nobody planned to; it just happened. Nil made tea in their mother's old teapot. Kemal turned off his phone. Sinan found their father's old guitar in the cupboard under the stairs, tuned it, and played the song their father used to sing when they were children, the one about the boat and the moon. They all knew the words.

They did not sell the house that year. In the end, they agreed on a compromise: Nil would live there, Kemal would help pay for the repairs, and Sinan would use the large front room for music lessons for children in the neighbourhood.

They still argue. Kemal still talks about property values. But once a year, on their father's birthday, all three of them sit around the kitchen table, and somebody, eventually, picks up the guitar.
