The Night Bus
B1

Hasan had driven the night bus, line N7, for eleven years. It left the city centre at midnight, crossed the bridge, drove through the sleeping suburbs, and came back at five in the morning. Most people thought it was a lonely job. Hasan didn't. He knew all his passengers.

There was the nurse who got on at the hospital at 12:20 every night, still in her uniform, and fell asleep immediately. Hasan always woke her up gently at her stop. There was the baker who started work at 2 a.m. and always brought Hasan a warm simit on Fridays. There was the student who studied with a heavy textbook on her knees, moving her lips silently as she read. And there was the old man who never got off.

The old man got on at the first stop, sat in the third row by the window, and rode the whole route, from beginning to end. He never spoke. When the bus returned to the centre at five, he got off, said "Thank you" quietly, and walked away.

For months, Hasan wondered about him. Was he homeless? Did he have insomnia? He was always clean and well dressed, with a grey coat and a hat.

One very cold night in January, the bus broke down on the bridge. While they waited for the mechanic, Hasan made tea in the small thermos he always carried and offered a cup to the old man.

"You ride this bus every night," Hasan said. "Can I ask why?"

The old man held the cup in both hands for a while. "My wife and I used to drive this route," he finally said. "She was a bus driver too. Line N7, for twenty-five years. I used to sit in the third row and keep her company. When she retired, we still took this bus sometimes, just to see the city at night. The bridge lights. The empty streets."

He looked out at the dark river.

"She died last spring. The house is very quiet at night. Here, I don't feel alone. I can almost hear her telling me about every passenger."

Hasan didn't know what to say. So he said the truth. "I think I knew her," he said. "When I started, there was an older driver who trained me. Leman Hanım. She told me to always wake up sleeping passengers gently, and that every passenger has a story."

The old man's eyes filled with tears. "That was her," he said. "That was exactly her."

The mechanic arrived, the bus started again, and they crossed the rest of the bridge in silence.

After that night, the old man didn't sit in the third row anymore. He sat in the front seat, next to Hasan, and they talked all the way through the empty city. He told Hasan stories about the old route, the old passengers, and the woman who had loved this bus.

And on Fridays, the baker started bringing two simits.
