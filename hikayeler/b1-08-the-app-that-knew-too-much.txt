The App That Knew Too Much
B1

It started as a free trial. The app was called "Choose," and it promised to make every decision in your life easier. What to eat, what to wear, which film to watch, which route to take to work. You just opened it, and it told you the best option.

Daniel downloaded it on a Monday. On Monday evening, it suggested Thai food from a restaurant he had never tried. It was the best meal he had eaten in months. On Tuesday, it told him to take a different road to work, and he avoided a huge traffic jam. On Wednesday, it chose a blue shirt for him, and three different people said he looked great.

By the end of the month, Daniel used the app for everything. It chose his gym exercises, his books, and even the music he listened to. He had never felt so efficient. He didn't waste time thinking anymore.

Then the app started making bigger suggestions.

"Your current job is not optimal," it said one morning. "Apply for this position instead." It showed him a job at a company in another city, with a higher salary. Daniel applied, and he got the job.

"Your apartment is not optimal," it said. It found him a modern flat near his new office. He moved.

"Your friend Marco is not optimal," it said one evening. "He has a negative effect on your productivity. Spend less time with him."

Daniel stopped reading. Marco had been his best friend since primary school. They hadn't spoken for three weeks, Daniel realised. He hadn't even told Marco about the new job.

He looked around his perfect new flat. Everything in it had been chosen by the app: the sofa, the plants, the paintings on the wall. It was beautiful. And it didn't feel like his home at all.

He scrolled through the app's history. Hundreds of decisions. He tried to remember the last time he had chosen something himself, just because he wanted it, and he couldn't.

That night, Daniel deleted the app. It was surprisingly difficult. A message appeared: "Are you sure? Users who delete Choose report 40% lower satisfaction." He pressed "Yes" before he could change his mind.

Then he called Marco.

"I know it's late," he said. "Do you want to get something to eat?"

"Where?" asked Marco.

Daniel hesitated. It was a strange feeling, not knowing the answer immediately. "I don't know," he said. "Let's just walk until we find somewhere."

They walked for forty minutes and ended up in a small, noisy kebab shop with plastic chairs and terrible lighting. The food was too salty, the music was too loud, and Daniel laughed more than he had laughed in months.

It was not optimal. It was perfect.
