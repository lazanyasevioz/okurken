The Voice
B2

The phone call came at 3:14 in the morning, and it was Aylin's daughter.

"Mum? Mum, I'm in trouble." Defne's voice was shaking, broken by sobs. "I had an accident with the car. A man is hurt. The police are here. Please, I need money for a lawyer right now, they won't let me —"

Then another voice took the phone: a calm, professional man who introduced himself as a solicitor. He explained that Defne had been arrested, that the situation was serious, and that a payment of five thousand euros was needed immediately to arrange bail. He gave her bank details. He told her not to call anyone, because it might make things worse for her daughter.

Aylin was already reaching for her laptop. Her heart was pounding so hard that she could barely breathe. Defne was twenty, studying in another city. She had passed her driving test only six months ago. Of course this could happen. Of course.

Her fingers were on the keyboard when something stopped her.

It wasn't anything logical. It was a tiny detail. When Defne was frightened, she always called her "Anne" — the Turkish word for mother — never "Mum." She had done it since she was a little girl, every time she fell off her bike or had a nightmare. It was the language of her fear.

The voice on the phone had said "Mum" three times.

"Can I speak to my daughter again?" Aylin asked.

"I'm afraid that's not possible right now," the man said smoothly. "Time is very important, madam."

Aylin put the phone down on the table, still connected, and picked up her mobile. She called Defne's number. It rang four times. Then a sleepy, confused voice answered.

"Anne? What's wrong? It's three in the morning."

Defne was in her bed, in her student flat, perfectly safe. She had never left home that night.

When Aylin picked up the other phone again, the line was dead.

The next day, she went to the police. The officer who took her statement was not surprised. "It's called voice cloning," he explained. "They only need a few seconds of someone's voice: a video on social media is enough. Then software can make that voice say anything. We've had eleven cases this month. Most people pay."

Defne had posted dozens of videos online: holiday clips, birthday messages, a cooking video with her friends. Somewhere, a stranger had collected those cheerful fragments of her voice and turned them into a weapon.

That evening, mother and daughter had a long video call. Defne was shocked and angry, and then embarrassed, and then quiet.

"So how did you know?" she asked eventually. "The voice was mine. You said it sounded exactly like me."

"It did," said Aylin. "But you called me 'Mum.' You never call me Mum when you're scared."

Defne laughed, a little shakily. Then they made a decision together. They chose a family password: a strange, silly word that no software could guess, that would never appear in any video. If either of them ever called the other in an emergency, they would say the word first. No word, no money.

Aylin told her sister about it, and her sister told her friends. Within a month, half of Aylin's extended family had their own secret words.

Aylin still thinks about that night sometimes. About how close she came to pressing the button. About how technology can copy a voice almost perfectly, but not the small, private habits that make that voice belong to someone you love.

The machines can learn how we sound, she thinks. They can't yet learn who we are.
