The Night Ferry
B2

The storm had delayed the ferry by four hours, and by the time it finally left the port, the passenger lounge had the atmosphere of a waiting room where everyone had given up waiting. Most people were asleep across rows of plastic seats. A few were staring at their phones, which had no signal.

At a corner table, four strangers had ended up sitting together simply because it was the only table left. There was an elderly man with a walking stick, a young woman with a large guitar case, a businessman in an expensive but crumpled suit, and Nadia, a medical student going home for her grandmother's eightieth birthday.

For the first hour, they ignored each other politely. Then the ship started to roll heavily, a tray of coffee cups crashed to the floor behind the bar, and the young woman with the guitar laughed nervously and said, "Well, if we're going to sink, we might as well know each other's names."

Her name was Lena. She was a musician, she said, on her way to her first real concert, in a theatre with eight hundred seats. She was terrified.

The businessman introduced himself as Victor. He said he worked in finance, travelled constantly, and hadn't been home for more than a week at a time in five years.

The old man was called Mr Ferreira. He said he was a retired fisherman returning to the island where he was born, sixty years after he had left it.

When it was her turn, Nadia hesitated, then told the truth: that she wasn't sure she wanted to be a doctor anymore, and that she hadn't told anyone, and that she was afraid of disappointing her family.

The storm continued, and so did the conversation. Something about the darkness, the noise of the waves and the fact that they would never see each other again made them more honest than they would normally be. Lena admitted that she sometimes wished she'd chosen a safer career. Victor admitted that his teenage son had stopped answering his messages. Mr Ferreira told them about a girl he had loved on the island when he was eighteen, and how he had left without saying goodbye because he was too proud.

At about four in the morning, Nadia noticed something. Mr Ferreira's story kept changing in small ways. The name of his village. The year he had left. The colour of the girl's dress. She said nothing, but she watched him more carefully.

Just before dawn, the ferry entered calm water, and a grey coastline appeared through the windows. People started to wake up and collect their bags. Lena and Victor exchanged numbers. Lena promised to send Nadia a recording of her concert.

Nadia found herself next to Mr Ferreira on the stairs to the car deck. "Your story," she said quietly. "It wasn't true, was it?"

The old man stopped. For a moment, she thought he was going to be angry. Instead, he smiled.

"No," he said. "I'm not a fisherman. I was a teacher in the city for forty years. I have never been to this island. My wife died in the spring, and I take this ferry sometimes because I can't sleep, and because strangers in a storm tell each other the truth." He shrugged. "I don't have any interesting truths of my own. So I borrow other people's stories, and I listen."

"Then why tell me?"

"Because you noticed," he said. "Most people don't. You'd make a good doctor, I think. Good doctors notice when the story doesn't fit."

The doors opened, and the cold morning air came in. Mr Ferreira lifted his walking stick in a small salute and disappeared into the crowd.

Nadia stood on the deck for a long time, watching the island come closer. Then she took out her phone, which finally had a signal, and called her mother.

"I need to talk to you about something," she said. "But first — tell Grandma happy birthday. I'm almost home."
