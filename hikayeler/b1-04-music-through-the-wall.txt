Music Through the Wall
B1

When Irem moved into her new flat, she thought it was perfect. It was small but bright, close to the university, and surprisingly cheap. Then, on the first night, at exactly eleven o'clock, the piano started.

It came from the flat next door, through the thin wall behind her bed. It was always the same piece of music: slow, beautiful, and never finished. The pianist played the first part perfectly, then made a mistake at the same place every time, stopped, and started again from the beginning.

The first night, Irem thought it was charming. By the fifth night, she was going crazy. She had exams, and she couldn't sleep.

On Saturday, she knocked on her neighbour's door, ready to complain. An old man opened it. He was very thin and wore a cardigan with holes in the elbows. Behind him, she could see a small living room, almost empty except for an old piano and a photograph of a smiling woman.

"I'm sorry to bother you," Irem began, "but the piano at night—"

"Oh no," the man said. His face fell. "I didn't think anyone could hear. I'm so sorry. I'll stop."

Something in his voice made her ask, "What is the piece you play?"

He was silent for a moment. Then he invited her in and made her a cup of tea. His name was Mr Halil. The woman in the photograph was his wife, Nermin. She had been a piano teacher, and she had written that piece of music for him on their wedding day, fifty years ago. She used to play it every night at eleven o'clock. She had died the previous winter.

"I never learned to play properly," he said. "She always said she would teach me, and I always said there was time. Now I'm trying to learn it alone. But I can never get past the middle."

Irem looked at the sheet music on the piano. The notes were handwritten in pencil. She had played the violin since she was six.

"Can I try something?" she asked.

She went back to her flat and returned with her violin. She played the difficult part slowly, while Mr Halil watched her fingers. Then they tried it together, again and again, all afternoon.

Two weeks later, at eleven o'clock, Irem lay in bed and listened. The piano began, slow and careful. It reached the difficult part, hesitated, and then continued, all the way to the end.

Through the wall, she heard an old man laughing. And then, very softly, crying.

After that, she never needed an alarm clock to know it was eleven.
