The Blackout
B1

At 8:43 on a Tuesday evening in July, the whole city went dark.

Selim was in the middle of an online game when his screen went black. At first, he thought it was his computer. Then he noticed the silence. No fridge humming. No air conditioning. No television from the flat next door. He looked out of the window: every building, every street light, every advertisement was dark. Only the headlights of cars were moving in the streets below.

His phone had forty percent battery, but the mobile network was overloaded. Messages wouldn't send. The news websites wouldn't load. Nobody knew what had happened.

His parents were away for the weekend, so Selim was alone. He was seventeen and not afraid of the dark, but the silence was strange. He found some candles in the kitchen and lit one. Then, because it was very hot inside, he went up to the roof of the building.

He wasn't the only one. Almost all of his neighbours were already there. Mrs Ayten from the fifth floor had brought a radio that worked with batteries. The family from the second floor had brought a big bottle of lemonade and plastic cups. Two little children were running around with torches, and a young couple he had never spoken to were sitting on a blanket.

The radio said that there was a problem with a power station outside the city and that the electricity would probably come back in a few hours. Everybody relaxed a little.

Then someone said, "Look up."

Selim looked up, and for a moment he couldn't speak.

He had lived in the city all his life. He had seen maybe ten or twenty stars on a clear night, never more. Now the sky was full of them, thousands and thousands, so many that they looked like dust. A wide, pale band stretched across the whole sky from one side to the other.

"That's the Milky Way," said the old man from the top floor, who used to be a sailor. "That's our galaxy. It's always there. We just can't see it because of the lights."

For the next three hours, nobody went inside. The sailor showed them how to find the North Star and told them how sailors used it to navigate before GPS. The children counted shooting stars. Mrs Ayten found an old song on the radio, and some people started singing. Selim talked to the young couple, who turned out to be music students, and they promised to teach him the guitar.

At 11:52, the lights came back. All at once, the buildings, the streets and the advertisements lit up, and the stars disappeared as if someone had switched them off. People cheered, but a little sadly. One by one, they went back downstairs.

Selim stayed on the roof a little longer, looking at the empty orange sky.

The next week, he bought a small telescope with his savings. And on the first Tuesday of every month, the neighbours meet on the roof, turn off their phones, and wait for the stars.
