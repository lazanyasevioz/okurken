The Interpreter
B2

Melis had been working as a hospital interpreter for three years, and she had developed a rule: translate the words, not the feelings. Her job was to be a bridge between doctors and patients who didn't share a language, and a bridge, she believed, shouldn't have opinions about the people crossing it.

Most days, the rule served her well. She translated questions about allergies and symptoms, explanations of medicines, instructions for after an operation. She was precise, quick and invisible, exactly as a good interpreter should be.

Then, one grey Thursday in November, she was called to the oncology department.

The patient was a man in his sixties called Yusuf. He had arrived in the country two years earlier, after fleeing a war that had taken his home, his job as an engineer, and most of his family. His only son lived in Germany, and his English consisted of perhaps twenty words. He had been having tests for several weeks.

The doctor, a tired but kind woman called Dr Hargreaves, explained the situation to Melis in the corridor before they went in. The cancer had spread. There was no realistic treatment. They could make him comfortable, but he probably had a few months left.

"I'd like to be direct but gentle," the doctor said. "Please tell me if I'm going too fast."

They went in. Yusuf was sitting very straight in a chair by the window, wearing a carefully ironed shirt, as if he had dressed for an important meeting. He smiled politely at Melis and thanked her for coming.

The doctor began to speak, and Melis translated, sentence by sentence. She kept her voice neutral and professional. She watched Yusuf's face as he understood, slowly, what he was being told. He didn't cry. He nodded, asked two precise questions about timing, and wrote the answers down in a small notebook.

Then he said something, and Melis hesitated for the first time in three years.

"Please tell the doctor," he said, "that I am grateful. In my country, I would not have been treated so well. And please ask her — is it possible to stay alive until June? My son's daughter will be born in June. I would like to hold her once."

Melis translated the words. But her voice broke on the last sentence, and she couldn't hide it.

Dr Hargreaves was quiet for a moment. Then she sat down, so that her eyes were at the same level as Yusuf's, and she answered him honestly: it was possible, but she couldn't promise it. They would do everything they could.

After the appointment, Melis went to the staff bathroom and cried for ten minutes. She was ashamed. She had broken her own rule. She had stopped being invisible.

The next week, she received a message through the hospital. Yusuf had specifically asked for her to interpret at all his future appointments.

"He says," the nurse explained, "that you were the only person who translated what he meant, not only what he said."

Over the following months, Melis was there for every appointment. Gradually, she learned about Yusuf's life: the bridges he had designed, the garden he had lost, the granddaughter he had never met. She learned that a good interpreter is not always invisible. Sometimes, the most accurate translation includes the voice breaking.

In June, Yusuf's son flew in from Germany with his wife and their two-week-old daughter. Melis was not needed that day — everybody in the room spoke the same language. But afterwards, Yusuf sent her a photograph: an old man in a carefully ironed shirt, holding a very small baby, smiling with his whole face.

On the back, he had written one word in English. It was not one of his original twenty. He had learned it especially for her.

"Bridge."
