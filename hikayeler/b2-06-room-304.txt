Room 304
B2

Every year on the seventeenth of October, an elderly woman checked into Room 304 of the Grand Palace Hotel for exactly one night.

Kerem had worked as the night receptionist at the hotel for six years, and she had come every single year. She always booked months in advance and always requested the same room, even when it was being renovated and they offered her a free upgrade to a suite. She arrived at seven in the evening, alone, with one small suitcase. She ordered the same thing from room service: a pot of tea, two cups, and a slice of chocolate cake with two forks. She checked out at seven the next morning, left a generous tip, and said goodbye politely to whoever was at the desk.

The staff had developed theories, as hotel staff always do. The housekeepers thought she was a widow remembering her honeymoon. The chef was convinced she was a famous writer who had written her first novel in that room. The manager suspected she was a hotel inspector and made sure Room 304 was always perfect in October.

Kerem didn't have a theory. He just noticed the details: the two cups, the two forks, the way she always looked at the lift for a long moment before she stepped into it, as if she was expecting someone to come out.

This year, she didn't arrive at seven.

At eight o'clock, Kerem checked the booking. It was still there. At nine, he called the phone number on her reservation. A young man answered.

"I'm calling from the Grand Palace Hotel," Kerem said carefully. "We have a reservation for Mrs Sevim Aksoy this evening, and we wanted to check that she is all right."

There was a long silence. Then the young man said, "I'm her grandson. My grandmother passed away three weeks ago. I'm sorry — we didn't know about the reservation. We're still going through her things."

Kerem expressed his condolences and was about to cancel the booking when the grandson asked, "Can I ask — did she stay there often?"

"Every year," said Kerem. "Same date. Same room."

Another silence. "Would it be strange," the young man said slowly, "if I came tonight? I think I'd like to understand."

He arrived just before eleven: a tall, tired man in his thirties, carrying a cardboard box. Kerem gave him the key to Room 304 and, without being asked, ordered a pot of tea, two cups and a slice of chocolate cake with two forks.

At two in the morning, the grandson came down to the empty lobby and sat on the sofa opposite the reception desk. He had a small bundle of letters in his hands.

"I found these in the box," he said. "They're from a man called Ali. He worked here, in this hotel, in 1968 — as a lift operator. My grandmother was staying here with her parents. She was nineteen. They met in the lift."

He turned a letter over in his hands.

"They fell in love in one week. Her family didn't approve; he was poor, and they had already chosen someone else for her. On the seventeenth of October, they planned to run away together. He was supposed to meet her in Room 304 at midnight. He never came. She waited all night. Her family took her home the next day, and two years later she married my grandfather."

"What happened to him?" Kerem asked quietly.

"The last letter is from his sister. He had been in a traffic accident on the way to the hotel that evening. He died two days later. My grandmother only found out a month afterwards." He paused. "She was married to my grandfather for forty-six years. I think they were happy. But every year, she came back here, and she waited for him one more time."

Kerem thought about the lift, and the way she always looked at it before stepping inside.

At seven in the morning, the grandson checked out. He left the letters with Kerem, asking him to keep them at the hotel, "where they belong."

Kerem still works the night shift. And every year on the seventeenth of October, although nobody has booked it, Room 304 is kept empty. At midnight, Kerem takes the lift up to the third floor, opens the door for a moment, and then closes it again.

Just in case anyone is still waiting.
