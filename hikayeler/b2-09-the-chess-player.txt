The Chess Player
B2

Everyone in the park knew Mr Vasiliev. Every afternoon, whatever the weather, he sat at the same stone table near the fountain with a battered wooden chessboard and a cardboard sign that said: "Beat me and win 50 lira. Lose and pay 10."

He was about seventy, with a thick white beard, an old military coat and a habit of humming opera while he played. People said he had once been a famous player in the Soviet Union. People also said he had been a spy, a circus performer and a professor of mathematics. He never confirmed or denied anything. He just set up the pieces and waited.

Hardly anybody beat him. Over the years, students, businessmen, tourists and even a few serious club players had sat down at his table, confident and smiling. Most of them left twenty minutes later, ten lira poorer and slightly confused about what had happened.

Selin was twelve when she first sat down opposite him. She had learned chess from a phone app during the pandemic, and she was the best player in her school, which, she admitted, wasn't saying much.

She lost in eleven moves.

She came back the next day and lost in fifteen. The day after that, she lasted twenty-three moves. Each time, she paid her ten lira from her pocket money, and each time, Mr Vasiliev took the coins without a word.

After two weeks, she had no pocket money left. She came to the park anyway and stood next to his table, watching other people lose.

"No money today?" he asked, without looking up from the board.

"No."

He grunted. "Then sit. Today we play for free. But you have to explain every move before you make it. Out loud."

It was the strangest lesson Selin had ever had. Before every move, Mr Vasiliev made her say why she was doing it. When her reason was bad, he simply said, "No," and waited until she found a better one. When it was good, he said nothing, which she slowly learned was the highest compliment he could give.

"You play too fast," he told her. "You see one good idea and you fall in love with it. A good player is suspicious of good ideas. Especially her own."

The lessons continued all summer, and then through the autumn. Gradually, Selin understood that Mr Vasiliev was not just teaching her chess. He was teaching her how to think: how to slow down, how to imagine what the other person wanted, how to stay calm when everything was going wrong. She started to use the same approach in her maths exams, in arguments with her parents, even in her first real friendship problems at school.

She also learned a little about him. He had indeed been a strong player once, though never famous. He had come to Turkey thirty years ago, after the Soviet Union collapsed, and worked as a night watchman in a factory until he retired. His wife had died, and his son lived in Canada. The chess table, she realised, was not really about money. It was about having a reason to talk to people every day.

In the spring, Selin entered the national youth chess championship. She did not win, but she came fourth, out of more than three hundred players, and she received a small silver medal.

She brought it to the park the next day. Mr Vasiliev looked at it for a long time, turning it over in his large, cold fingers.

"Fourth," he said. "Good. Not too good. Too good is dangerous at your age." But he was smiling under his beard.

Then he set up the pieces.

"Fifty lira if you win," he said.

They played for almost two hours. A small crowd gathered around the stone table. Selin explained none of her moves out loud; she didn't need to anymore. In the end, with only a few pieces left on the board, she saw it: a quiet, unexpected move that she was immediately suspicious of, and checked three times, and then played.

Mr Vasiliev studied the board for a full minute. Then he tipped over his king, reached into his old coat, and placed fifty lira on the table.

She didn't take it. "Keep it," she said. "Pay for my next lesson."

He laughed, a loud, rough laugh that made the pigeons fly up from the fountain. Then he took down the cardboard sign, turned it over, and wrote on the other side in black marker:

"Free lessons. Explain every move."
