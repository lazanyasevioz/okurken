Wrong Number
B2

The first message arrived on a Wednesday afternoon, while Elena was sitting in a boring meeting at work.

"Happy birthday, my darling. I bought the blue scarf you liked. I'll bring it on Sunday. Don't let them give you the terrible soup. Love, Dad."

Elena stared at her phone. It wasn't her birthday, her father had died years ago, and she didn't own a blue scarf. Clearly, someone had the wrong number.

She typed a polite reply: "I'm sorry, I think you have the wrong number. I hope your daughter has a lovely birthday!"

The answer came a few minutes later. "Oh dear. I'm so sorry. My daughter changed her phone, and I must have written the new number wrong. My eyes are not what they were. Thank you for telling me, and I'm sorry to have bothered you."

That should have been the end of it. But that evening, Elena kept thinking about the message. The terrible soup. Who gives someone terrible soup on their birthday? She wrote again, before she could stop herself.

"I hope you don't mind me asking — is your daughter in hospital?"

The reply came quickly, as if he had been waiting. His name was Arthur, he was eighty-three, and his daughter Rose was in a rehabilitation centre after a stroke. She was recovering slowly. He visited every Sunday, which required two buses and a long walk, because she was the only family he had left.

Over the next few weeks, the messages continued. It began with small things. Arthur sent her a photograph of the blue scarf. Elena told him about her boring meetings. He asked her advice about using the video function on his phone so he could call Rose during the week. She sent him step-by-step instructions, with screenshots and arrows, and he managed it on the fourth attempt and sent her a message entirely in capital letters: "IT WORKED. SHE LAUGHED."

Elena lived alone in a city where she had moved for work two years earlier. She had colleagues, but not really friends. Her own mother lived on the other side of the world, and they spoke once a month, politely, about the weather. She found herself looking forward to Arthur's messages more than she expected. He was funny, in an old-fashioned, gentle way. He had been a train driver for forty years and knew everything about railways, which was not a subject she had ever cared about, but she found herself reading his long explanations with interest.

In December, Arthur wrote that Rose was going to leave the rehabilitation centre and come home for Christmas, for the first time in a year. He was nervous. The house was not ready. He couldn't reach the high shelves to get the decorations down, and he wanted everything to be perfect.

Elena looked at the address he had mentioned once in passing. It was forty minutes away by train.

She had never met him. He could have been anybody. Her friends at work, if she had told them, would have said it was crazy. She went anyway, on a cold Saturday morning, with a box of biscuits and a little nervous feeling in her stomach.

The door was opened by a small, thin man with enormous glasses and a cardigan buttoned incorrectly. He looked at her for a moment, confused. Then his whole face changed.

"Wrong number," he said, and laughed, and held out both his hands.

They spent the whole day decorating the house. Elena climbed the ladder; Arthur gave instructions and told stories about every decoration — the glass bird his wife had bought in 1971, the wooden star Rose had made at school. They ate the biscuits and drank too much tea.

On Christmas Day, Elena received a video call. It was Arthur and Rose, sitting together in the decorated living room. Rose's speech was still slow, and one side of her face didn't move properly, but her eyes were bright.

"Dad says you're his favourite mistake," Rose said slowly.

Elena laughed, and was surprised to find that she was also crying a little.

She still has the wrong number saved in her phone. She has never changed the name. It still says, simply: "Wrong Number." It has become the most right thing in her contacts list.
