Home Again
B2

When Necati left his hometown in 1994, he was twenty-one years old, with one suitcase, a phone number written on a piece of paper and a promise to his mother that he would be back in two years.

He came back thirty years later.

In between, he had built a life in Germany that he could never have imagined as a boy. He had started as a factory worker, learned the language at night school, trained as an electrician, and eventually opened his own small company with eleven employees. He had married, raised two children who spoke German better than Turkish, and divorced. He had attended his mother's funeral by video call during the pandemic, because the borders were closed, which was the thing in his life he regretted most.

Now he was retired, and his children were adults with their own lives, and he had finally run out of reasons not to come home.

The first thing he noticed was that home had disappeared.

His neighbourhood, which he remembered as a collection of two-storey houses with gardens, fig trees and narrow streets where children played football until dark, had been replaced by tall apartment blocks and a shopping centre. The bakery where he had bought bread every morning for his mother was now a phone shop. The school he had attended had been demolished. He walked around for an hour, looking for anything familiar, and felt more like a tourist than he ever had in Germany.

He spent the first week in a hotel, feeling foolish and lonely. His Turkish, which he had always spoken at home, suddenly seemed old-fashioned; young people smiled at some of his expressions. Prices had changed, habits had changed, even the way people queued at the bus stop had changed. In Germany, he had always been "the Turkish one." Here, he slowly realised, people saw him as "the German one." He belonged to neither place completely.

On the eighth day, almost ready to give up and book a flight back, he took a wrong turn on his way to the hotel and found himself in a small street that had somehow survived. There were three old houses, a crooked fig tree, and a tiny tea garden with plastic chairs. An old man was sitting alone at one of the tables, reading a newspaper.

Necati sat down and ordered tea. The old man looked at him over his newspaper for a long time. Then he folded it slowly.

"You're Saadet Hanım's son," he said. "The one who went to Germany."

Necati was so surprised that he spilled his tea. The man's name was Rıfat. He had been his mother's neighbour for forty years, and he had known Necati as a skinny boy who broke windows with his football.

"Your mother talked about you every day," Rıfat said. "Every single day. She had a box of your letters. She read them to anyone who sat still long enough. I think I know more about your company in Germany than you do."

Necati didn't know what to say. He had always believed that his mother had been disappointed in him for not coming back, for missing the important moments, for not being there at the end.

"She wasn't angry," Rıfat said, as if he could read his face. "She was proud. She just missed you. Those are different things."

They talked until the tea garden closed. Rıfat told him stories about his mother's last years, about the neighbourhood, about who had died and who had moved away and who was still there. The next day, he introduced Necati to the other old neighbours who remained, scattered across the new apartment blocks. Several of them remembered him. One woman cried and hugged him as if he were her own son.

Necati did not book the flight back. He rented a small flat in one of the three old houses on Rıfat's street. He spends his mornings repairing the electrical problems of everybody in the neighbourhood for free, his afternoons in the tea garden, and his evenings on video calls with his children in Germany, who have promised to visit in the summer.

He has accepted that the home he left no longer exists. But he has discovered that home was never really the buildings. It was the people who remembered you, and the people you were willing to remember.

On the wall of his new flat, there is one framed photograph: his mother, standing under a fig tree, holding a letter from Germany and smiling.
