The Island Doctor
B2

Dr Leyla Arslan had chosen the island job precisely because nothing ever happened there.

After eight exhausting years in the emergency department of a huge city hospital, she had applied for the position of the only doctor on a small island in the Aegean with fewer than nine hundred permanent residents. Her days now consisted of blood pressure checks, minor cuts from fishing hooks, and long conversations with elderly patients who mostly wanted company. In summer, there were sunburnt tourists. In winter, there was almost nothing at all.

Then came the storm.

It arrived on a February night with almost no warning, the worst in thirty years. The ferry was cancelled. The wind was so strong that the helicopter from the mainland couldn't take off. The electricity on the island failed at nine o'clock, and the clinic switched to its old, unreliable generator.

At ten fifteen, a fisherman called Hüseyin carried his sixteen-year-old son, Barış, into the clinic. The boy was grey, sweating, and bent over in pain. He had been complaining of stomach ache since the afternoon.

Leyla examined him and felt her own stomach drop. It was appendicitis, and from the signs, the appendix was about to burst — or perhaps already had. In the city, he would have been in surgery within an hour. Here, she had no surgeon, no operating theatre, no anaesthetist, and no way of getting him to the mainland until the storm passed, which could take a day or more.

She knew what a burst appendix could mean without surgery. She did not say it out loud.

She tried to call the regional hospital. The mobile network was down. The landline crackled and died. Then she remembered the satellite phone, which had been sitting in a cupboard since she arrived, still in its box. She had been trained to use it once, briefly, two years ago.

It took her twenty minutes to get a signal. Finally, a voice answered: Dr Okan Yıldız, the chief surgeon at the mainland hospital. She explained the situation as clearly as she could. There was a pause.

"You'll have to operate," he said. "I'll talk you through it."

Leyla had not performed surgery since medical school, more than twelve years earlier. She told him so. "I know," he said calmly. "But you're the only one there. And I'll be with you the whole time."

What followed were the longest three hours of Leyla's life. Hüseyin held a torch when the generator flickered. The island's retired nurse, a seventy-year-old woman called Fatma who had been woken by her neighbour, arrived in her dressing gown and prepared the instruments as if she had done it yesterday. The satellite phone was taped to a shelf, set to loudspeaker, and Dr Yıldız's calm voice filled the room, describing each step, asking her what she saw, telling her where to cut and where not to.

Twice the connection dropped, and Leyla had to stand completely still, her hands inside a patient, waiting for the voice to return. The second time, it took four minutes. They were the most frightening four minutes she had ever experienced.

But the operation worked. At ten past two in the morning, Leyla closed the last stitch. Barış's blood pressure was stable. On the other end of the line, a tired surgeon said, "Well done, doctor. Now go and sit down before you fall down."

She sat on the floor of the clinic, still in her gloves, and shook for twenty minutes. Fatma brought her a glass of sweet tea and didn't say a word.

The storm passed two days later, and a helicopter took Barış to the mainland, where the doctors said that the surgery had been performed "unconventionally, but successfully." He made a full recovery.

The story spread around the island, of course, and then beyond it. A national newspaper wanted to interview her. Leyla refused. She didn't feel like a hero. She felt like a woman who had been very lucky, and who had been helped by a calm voice, a retired nurse and a frightened father with a torch.

But she did make one change. She wrote a long, detailed report about the night, and sent it to the Ministry of Health. Six months later, the island clinic received a new generator, a permanent satellite connection, and funding for an annual emergency training course for doctors on remote islands.

Barış came to see her when he returned home. He brought a large fish, freshly caught, and a card he had obviously written with some help from his mother.

"Thank you for not being boring," it said.
