The Restorer
B2

The violin arrived at Aram's workshop in a plastic supermarket bag.

Aram Petrosyan had restored stringed instruments for thirty-five years, in a narrow, dusty workshop above a bakery in an old part of Istanbul. He had repaired violins worth more than houses, sent to him by famous orchestras and nervous collectors. He had also repaired cheap student instruments for children whose parents couldn't afford new ones, and he charged them almost nothing. He believed every instrument deserved to sing, whatever its price.

But he had never seen anything quite like this.

The man who brought it was a diver in his forties called Cengiz. He explained that he worked for a team of marine archaeologists exploring a shipwreck at the bottom of the Sea of Marmara: a passenger ship that had sunk in a storm in 1926, with more than a hundred people on board. Among the objects they had recovered was a wooden case, sealed with wax, which had protected its contents from the water better than anyone could have hoped.

Inside the case was the violin.

It was in terrible condition. The varnish was almost gone, the neck was cracked, and the wood had warped into strange shapes after nearly a century under the sea. Most restorers, Cengiz said, had told him it was impossible. One had suggested putting it in a museum as it was.

Aram turned it over slowly in his hands. Then he noticed something. Inside the body of the violin, visible through one of the sound holes, there was a small paper label. Most of the writing had faded, but he could read a few words in Armenian, his grandmother's language: a maker's name, a city, and a year — 1904.

"I'll try," he said.

The restoration took fourteen months. It was the most difficult work Aram had ever done. He had to dry the wood so slowly that it would not crack further, which alone took half a year. He had to rebuild parts of the neck and replace pieces of wood that had been eaten by sea creatures, finding old timber that matched the original as closely as possible. Every evening, he wrote detailed notes about each step, in case he made a mistake that someone after him would need to understand.

While he worked, Cengiz and the archaeologists searched the ship's records. The case had belonged to a passenger listed as "S. Manukyan, musician, age 23." With help from historians and an online community of descendants, they slowly put together a story. Sarkis Manukyan had been a young violinist travelling to a new job with an orchestra. He had been on his way to meet his fiancée, whose photograph, it turned out, had been tucked inside the lid of the case. He had not survived the sinking.

His fiancée, they discovered, had eventually married and had children. Her great-granddaughter, a woman named Anna, was now a music teacher living in France.

When the violin was finished, Aram tuned it carefully and drew the bow across the strings for the first time. The sound that came out was not perfect. It was slightly dark, slightly rough, like a voice that had been silent for a long time. But it was unmistakably alive.

Anna came to Istanbul in the spring. She was nervous; she had never played for an audience bigger than her students. The concert was held in a small church near the sea, not far from where the ship had sunk. Aram sat in the front row with Cengiz and the team of divers, all of them in their best clothes.

Anna played a simple piece: an old Armenian folk song that her great-grandmother used to sing, about a bird waiting for spring. The violin's dark, rough voice filled the church. Nobody moved.

Afterwards, she came over to Aram with tears in her eyes and tried to thank him. He shook his head.

"I didn't do anything," he said. "The violin waited a hundred years. I just helped it remember how to sing."

The violin now lives with Anna in France, and she plays it for her students every week. Inside the case, next to the faded photograph of a young woman, there is a new note in Aram's handwriting. It lists every repair, every piece of new wood, every step of the restoration.

At the end, it says: "Restored with patience. Please keep playing."
