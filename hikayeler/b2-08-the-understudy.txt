The Understudy
B2

For eleven months, Tara had been the understudy for the role of Lady Macbeth at the Royal Crown Theatre. That meant she knew every line, every movement and every costume change of the most important female role in the play. It also meant that, in eleven months and three hundred and twenty performances, she had never once stepped onto the stage.

The actress who played the role, the famous and terrifying Margaret Hale, had never missed a show. She had performed with a fever. She had performed with a broken toe. Once, it was rumoured, she had performed the day after her own wedding, and complained that the champagne at the reception had been too warm.

Tara spent most evenings in a small room backstage, listening to the performance through a speaker and silently mouthing the words along with Margaret. She was twenty-six. She had trained for four years at drama school. Her friends sometimes asked her how the show was going, and she found it difficult to explain that she was in it and not in it at the same time.

Then, at 6:15 on a Saturday evening, forty-five minutes before the curtain went up, the stage manager burst into her room.

"Margaret has food poisoning," he said. "She can't stand up. You're on."

The next forty-five minutes passed in a blur. Someone pushed her into the costume, which had been made for Margaret and was slightly too long. Someone else did her makeup in record time. The director appeared, looking pale, and gave her one piece of advice: "Don't try to be Margaret. Nobody can be Margaret. Just be very, very loud."

An announcement was made to the audience. Tara heard it through the speaker: "At this evening's performance, the role of Lady Macbeth will be played by Tara Quinn." She also heard the sound that followed — a long, disappointed groan from nine hundred people who had paid a lot of money to see a famous actress.

She stood in the dark at the side of the stage and seriously considered running out of the building.

Then her cue came, and she walked into the light.

What happened in the next three hours, she could never fully remember afterwards. She remembered the heat of the lights and the strange silence of a full theatre. She remembered forgetting one line in the second act and inventing something in roughly the right style, which nobody noticed. She remembered, in the famous sleepwalking scene, looking at her own hands and feeling something she had never felt in eleven months of rehearsals: that the words were not memorised anymore. They were simply hers.

When the play ended, there was a pause, a long pause, and Tara's heart sank. Then the applause started. It went on, and on, and people began to stand up.

In the dressing room afterwards, there were flowers, and the director hugging her, and the other actors shouting. And there was a note, which arrived by taxi at eleven o'clock, written in shaky handwriting on hotel paper:

"I listened to the whole thing on the stage manager's phone. You were not me. You were better at being you than I expected. Don't get used to it. I'll be back on Tuesday. — M.H."

Margaret Hale did come back on Tuesday, and played the role for another four months. Tara returned to the little room backstage. But something had changed. The reviews of her single performance had been extraordinary, and agents began to call.

A year later, Tara was cast as the lead in a new play at a different theatre. On her opening night, there was a large bouquet in her dressing room, with a note in the same shaky handwriting:

"Remember the understudy. She knows all your lines, and she is hoping you'll get food poisoning. Be kind to her anyway. — M.H."

Tara was. Every week, she made sure her own understudy got at least one chance to perform.
